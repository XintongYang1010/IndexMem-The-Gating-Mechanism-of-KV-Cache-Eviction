The Gating Mechanism of KV Cache Eviction

Hao Gu1*, Xintong Yang2*, Zhiwei Li3, Lei Zhu4, Jian Yang1, Qiyuan Zhu1, Dezhi Li1, Jingyuan Zhang5, Zhizhuo Kou1, Hao Wang6, Bei Liu1, Jiacheng Liu7, Sirui Han1†

Train a gate, on the model's own loss, to decide which KV entries the model can do without, and it learns to evict nothing. The reason sits in the denominator of the softmax. So does the fix.

Learned KV eviction is not a capacity problem. It is an objective problem.

1What eviction has to decide

Not whether an entry will be used again, but whether the rest of the cache can still supply it

Sparse attention selects the entries relevant to the current query and selects again at the next one. Eviction deletes: every later query loses the entry, and the question that will need it has not arrived yet. So eviction has to answer a different question: once this entry is gone, is its information still available elsewhere in the cache? Causal attention copies much of the past into later entries, which is why a sliding window sees beyond itself. An isolated fact, such as a password or a retrieval key, may live in one entry only.

Most eviction methods answer the first question with a proxy: SnapKV reads the attention of recent queries, KeyDiff asks whether a key is geometrically distinctive, TrimKV leans on token age. IndexMem++ scores each entry from its own hidden state, with no reference to a future query. The decision is made once, when an entry falls out of the budget, and the entry is physically deleted.

That score has no per-entry label to regress. The only supervision is the model's output, so the score enters attention as a gate inside the softmax, where lowering one entry hands its attention mass to the others, exactly as removing it would.

2Trained directly

The gate is unusable, and it is not a tuning problem

4.33RULER 8K with 75% of KV evicted, gate trained on the LM loss
70.01The same model with nothing evicted (Qwen3-0.6B)

A small MLP reads a score sj = log gj off each entry's hidden state, and the backbone stays frozen. Trained on the language-modeling loss, the scores come out nearly constant. The loss falls the whole time, so optimization is not what failed. A constant added to every logit cancels in the denominator, so attention stays exactly dense, and a backbone fitted to dense attention has its lowest loss there. The top-B cut then falls among near-ties and keeps an arbitrary set.

Existing learned-eviction mechanisms, the sparsity penalty in TrimKV and the relaxations of discrete decisions in DMC and DMS, work around this solution rather than remove it.

The no-op is not a floor the gate improves from. It is what the objective asks for.

3What makes it work

Three things, all read off the same denominator

Ablations in this section: Qwen3-0.6B, RULER 8K average, 75% of KV evicted, each arm trained for 100 steps. The dense model scores 70.01.

1Close the no-op, then change the teacher

Make "keep everything" unreachable, then distill from the full-KV model

Pinned entries and a fixed mass. Every eviction policy keeps a few attention sinks and a local window anyway. Hold their gates at 1 and they become a reference frame. The remaining entries share a total gate mass β far below their count, so constant scores can only scale all of them down together, by β/|E|, about 3% in the pilot. Dense attention is out of reach, and an entry has to outscore the others to stay. β is also the exact sparsity during training; no penalty or schedule is needed.

Distillation instead of next-token loss. The cross-entropy gradient on the output logits is the sum of two errors: the one eviction causes, and the one the frozen model already makes, which no gate can fix and which dominates at hard positions. Reweighting tokens scales both together. Distilling against the same model with full KV leaves only the first term.

2Across heads

Scores cannot be compared across heads, so retained attention mass splits the budget, online

Adding a constant to every score of one head changes nothing inside that head, so the level of a head's scores is never trained, and ranking two heads' scores together means nothing. The split has to come from a quantity the shift does not move: the attention mass each head retains.

The allocation is computed once, at the end of prefill, from the attention this input actually produced. Each head orders its evictable entries by score and accumulates the mass retained as entries are added on top of the pinned sinks and window. A shared target ρ* is found by bisection so that the per-head slot counts, each the smallest that reaches ρ*, sum to the layer budget; a floor of K/4 keeps any head from being starved. A head whose attention is spread across the context gets more slots, a head whose mass already sits on the sinks and window gets fewer. Nothing is fitted in advance, and decoding runs with these budgets held fixed.

3Recovery

Evicted information comes back inside the same softmax: centroid memory (CMP)

Eviction drops a share ρ of the attention mass, about 0.2 in our measurements. With oS and oE the normalized outputs over the retained and evicted entries:

odense=(1−ρ)oS+ρoE,ρ=ZEZS+ZE

Recovery needs both terms: the direction oE, which is the output the query would get from the evicted entries alone, and the share ρ, a ratio of softmax normalizers. A memory read after attention falls short on both. Its reconstruction of oE is poor, and even an exact oE would still need the mixing weights 1 − ρ and ρ, which it never sees and has to learn indirectly in output space. IndexMem++ keeps the evicted entries inside the softmax instead, as a small set of centroids.

It measures the direction only; the share ρ is not part of it, and for the centroids it comes from the softmax itself. Linear-state and TTT-style memories barely improve when made larger. Downstream, Qwen3-0.6B on Math500 with 512 entries per head goes from 48.00 to 52.00 with 64 centroid slots taken out of the same budget.

Capacity was not the bottleneck

A scorer that re-reads the past

A causal convolution, a recurrent state or prefix attention over the history grows the scorer from 7.5M to as much as 24.1M parameters. All three land within 1.2 points of the plain MLP; the hidden state already carries its context.

5What the trained gate keeps

Not a sliding window: early facts stay cached until they are used

Does the gate retain early context, or does it reduce to a sliding window? The control is a recency policy that keeps the most recent entries under the same per-head budgets and centroid slots. Both run with physical eviction on Qwen3-8B at K = 2,048, on long AIME25 reasoning traces and on software-engineering agent trajectories (SWE), fed token by token up to 8K.

Across layers

Adjacent layers retain similar positions, weighted Jaccard 0.512, and layers eight or more apart less so, 0.413; a random selection matched for each head's capacity and age profile gives 0.513 for adjacent layers. Taking the union over all 36 layers, an original entry survives somewhere for 99.9% of the selectable positions.

Across heads

Within a layer, two heads' retained sets overlap with Jaccard 0.372, against 0.212 for the matched random selection. In 8K snapshots the two heads with the largest budgets hold 50.2% of a layer's selectable capacity, where a uniform split would give 25%.

Retention is not specific to facts that are applied later: early facts with no observed later use are also kept, 111 of 111 at 4K and 109 of 111 at 8K. Full setup in Appendix J of the paper.

6Summary

Not a capacity problem. An objective problem.

Can a gate that learns only from the model's output, and never sees the next question, learn what to evict? Yes, once "keep everything" is no longer a solution the objective can reach.
